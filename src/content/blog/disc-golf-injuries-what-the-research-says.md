Most disc golf injuries are overuse injuries to the throwing arm, and they build up over months rather than arriving in one bad throw. Surveys put the share of players who've been injured at some point above 81 percent, with the elbow and shoulder leading the list. This post covers how common disc golf injuries are, which body parts they hit most, heat illness on summer rounds, warm-up routines, disc-strike safety on shared paths, and when to stop playing. It's general information, not medical advice. See a doctor for pain that persists.

## How Common Are Disc Golf Injuries?

More than 81% of surveyed disc golf players reported that they had sustained an injury from playing the sport. That figure comes from the Nelson et al. Survey of 883 players published in the *Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine* in 2015 (F149, F32, F209).

![Diagram: How Common Are Disc Golf Injuries?](/diagrams/napkin-753c3ffe8a.png)

The breakdown by body part, from the same study, is the part I would pay attention to (F1):

| Body part | Injuries reported |
| --- | --- |
| Elbow | 325 |
| Shoulder | 305 |
| Back | 218 |
| Knee | 199 |

### Why the numbers point to overuse

The Nelson study describes disc golf injuries as tied to "the repetitive forceful movements of throwing the discs" (F209). That matters because it tells you what kind of problem you're most likely to run into. A single bad throw can hurt you. The more common pattern is wear that builds across many rounds.

One detail backs this up. Of the players who reported an elbow injury, only 10.3% had injured that elbow before taking up disc golf (F6). The throwing motion itself looks like the new load.

### Read the 81% with some caution

Survey design inflates numbers like this. The Nelson authors note that injured players may have been more likely to respond, so the true share of players who get hurt is probably lower than 81% (F149). A smaller Danish survey of 105 players found 13.3% had an active injury at the time they answered, which is a different question and a much smaller sample.

I wouldn't treat 81% as your personal odds. I would treat it as a signal that disc golf isn't the free pass on your body that the low-impact reputation suggests. If you play often and throw hard, the repetitive load is real, and it usually shows up in the elbow or shoulder first.

## Most Common Disc Golf Injuries by Body Part

Elbow injuries top the list. In the Nelson survey of 715 injured players, 46 percent reported elbow injuries, with the shoulder close behind at 43 percent, according to Ultiworld Disc Golf's report on the study.

That order catches people off guard. Most assume throwing pain means shoulder trouble. The throw styles in the same research back it up: 86% of players throw backhand as their primary style, 13% forehand, and 1% overhead. Forehand throwers were more likely to sustain an elbow injury (P =.014).

### Injury types and other body parts

Table 2 of the Nelson study lists the most common self-reported diagnoses: strain or pulled muscle at 36.5%, sprain or ligament tear at 24.5%, and tendinitis at 22.0%. Spinal disc or nerve injury appeared in 6.7% of responses.

Back injuries (n = 218) and knee injuries (n = 199) also show up in the survey data. The surgery list includes meniscal, shoulder, spine, and foot or ankle procedures, so the ankle and spine are real concerns, just less frequent than the arm.

If your elbow hurts on forehand lines but not backhand, I'd treat that as a technique signal rather than a rest problem.

## Disc Golf Injury Prevention Strategies

Injuries happen in disc golf, same as any other throwing sport (F41). Warm-ups come up most often as the fix. I'd keep doing one, but I wouldn't expect it to do everything.

### What the evidence actually shows

That result surprises people, so here's my read: a warm-up is still worth your time, but the study tested one program against whatever players already did, not against no warm-up at all. A couple of practice putts isn't the same as five minutes of arm and trunk movement.

The Danish survey of 105 players found the median warm-up before playing was five minutes (F266), which is short for a throwing motion. I'd aim longer.

### Disc count and warm-up duration

Two associations stand out from the same Danish survey:

A 10-minute warm-up or longer showed an association with injury status (RR = 1.82; 95% CI 0.95 to 3.46) (F270). So did carrying 20 or more discs (RR = 1.85; 95% CI 0.96 to 3.54) (F270).

Both confidence intervals cross 1.0, so I wouldn't treat either as proof. My pick is to trim the bag anyway. Hauling 20 or more discs adds load to a shoulder that is already throwing, and if the association is real, the fix is free.

For the warm-up itself, I would skip the static stretching you see on tee pads and use dynamic movement through the throwing arc instead.

## Disc Golf and Heat Safety

Heat is the one hazard on the course that has nothing to do with your form. The National Weather Service and the CDC both publish heat guidance aimed at anyone exerting themselves outdoors. I would treat a summer round as an exertion event first and a game second.

### Why heat illness risk climbs

Risk rises with prolonged exertion in warm weather. A round of 9 or 18 baskets (F205) keeps you walking and throwing for a long stretch, often in full sun with little shade. Add humidity, and the body's ability to cool itself drops. The CDC guidance on heat illness separates the warning signs into stages, from heat cramps and heavy sweating up through heat exhaustion and heat stroke, and heat stroke is a medical emergency. I would learn that ladder before the round, not during it.

### What to do

- Drink before you are thirsty, and keep drinking through the round.
- Take breaks in shade, especially around the middle holes.
- Watch your cardmates, not just yourself. Confusion or slurred speech in a playing partner means stop and get help.
- Move the round to early morning or evening when the forecast is brutal.

If the usual advice doesn't fit, say you are on a medication that affects sweating or hydration, or you have already felt dizzy once that day. Then the answer isn't more water. It's to stop playing and get into air conditioning.

This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to a doctor about your own risk.

## Disc Strike Safety and When to Stop Playing

A disc leaves your hand faster than most people on a shared path expect, so the thrower owns the outcome. Before you throw, check the full flight line and the landing zone, not just the gap you are aiming through.

### Playing Near Other People

Shared-use paths, park loops and multi-use green space are the highest-risk places to throw. A forehand can reach speeds between 100 and 130 km/h, per the Austrian randomized controlled trial published in *Science and Sports*, and discs usually weigh between 150 and 180 g, according to Nelson and colleagues in the *Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine*. That's enough to hurt a walker, a cyclist or a dog before anyone reacts.

My rule is blunt, and it goes against the common advice to just yell "fore" and throw anyway. Never throw blind over a crest, around a corner or into a blind landing zone. If you can't see where the disc will land, walk forward until you can, or pick a different line. When a person or animal enters your line mid-run-up, stop the throw. A lost stroke costs nothing.

### When to Stop

Stop playing if you feel dizzy, nauseated, cramping or unusually weak in the heat. Stop immediately on sharp pain, and don't throw through persistent discomfort hoping it settles. Rest, cool down and hydrate, and see a medical professional if the pain or symptoms continue.

This article is general information, not medical advice. Talk to a qualified healthcare provider about your own situation.

## FAQ

### What is the 35 rule in disc golf?

No source in my research defines it either. If you heard it at your course, ask the person who said it which rule they mean. The PDGA rulebook is free to read, and that is the document that settles arguments. If someone tells you a 35 rule governs your lie, your stance or your score, ask them to point to the rule number.

### Is disc golf hard on your body?

It's easier on the body than most field sports, but injuries are common. In a survey of 883 disc golf players, more than 81% of respondents stated that they had sustained an injury playing disc golf, including injuries to the elbow, shoulder, back and knee (Nelson et al., Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine, 2015). A separate Danish study of 105 players reported that overuse injuries may be prevalent, owing to the repetitive forceful movements of throwing the discs (International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy). The sport is low-intensity and non-contact, so the load builds up over many throws rather than in one collision.

### Is disc golf growing or dying?

Growing, by every measure in the research. The number of disc golf courses built each year has increased by 6% to 14% annually, with nearly 3000 courses in the United States alone (Nelson et al., 2015). The same study reports that the PDGA currently has more than 20,000 active members, and a later paper puts the figure at over 130,000 active players who regularly participate in competitions (Thiel and Kleinhappl, Science and Sports). Growth in players tends to follow growth in courses, and both numbers are climbing.

### What's the worst injury you've had while playing disc golf?

I can't answer this one from my own rounds, and I wouldn't trust a stranger's story as medical guidance. What the research shows is where the serious cases land. Of 874 respondents asked about injury, 715 (81.8%) reported that they had sustained an injury that was due to disc golf (Nelson et al., 2015). Among the most serious injuries, 115 players stated that they had undergone surgery, with the most common surgeries performed including meniscal surgery, rotator cuff repair or other shoulder surgery, spine surgery, foot or ankle surgery, and knee scope (Nelson et al., 2015). Those are the outcomes worth avoiding.

### At what point do you take a disc out of your bag due to damage?

The research doesn't give a damage threshold, so I will give you my own rule instead. Retire a disc when the rim has a gouge you can catch a fingernail in, when a crack reaches the flight plate, or when the disc has warped enough that it no longer flies the line you rely on. A disc that behaves differently on every throw is a scoring problem, not a sentimental one. For me the test is simple: if I can't predict the flight, it comes out of the bag. Damage to discs isn't covered in the injury studies I read.
