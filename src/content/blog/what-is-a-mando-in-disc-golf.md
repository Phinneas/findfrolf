A mando in disc golf is a mandatory route, a rule that forces your disc to pass one specific side of a marked object like a tree, pole, or post. The word is shorthand for "mandatory," and a course designer or tournament director sets it so the hole plays the way they intended.

If you're asking what is a mando in disc golf the first time you spot an arrow on a tree, here's the short version. The arrow tells you which side to take. Miss it and you add a penalty throw.

Below I cover what a mando is, how to read the arrow, what happens when you miss one, how drop zones work, and whether mandos apply in casual rounds.

## What a Mando Is

A mando in frisbee golf means the same thing as a mando in disc golf. The word is shorthand for "mandatory," and it means a course designer or tournament director has mandated how your disc may legally pass a specific obstacle on the hole.

Most mandos force a throw to one side of a tree or pole. You'll also run into mandos that require going under, over, or between objects. Two mando objects working together make a double mando, and you must throw between them. A triple mando, like the one used at the U.S. Disc Golf Championship, forces a throw through an archway or gate-shaped set of objects. Left and right arrows cover the common case, but a down arrow means your disc has to travel below whatever the sign is attached to.

The rule to know is 804.01, Mandatory Routes, in the PDGA's Official Rules of Disc Golf. The PDGA's Rules School notes that the rules governing mandatory routes "were revised in 2022, so pay close attention to make sure you are up to date."

That revision matters because of the restricted plane. Rule 804.01.B defines it as an imaginary plane that usually extends in the direction opposite of where the mando forces you to throw. If the sign points left, the restricted plane sits on the right side of the object. The plane isn't a guess on your part. Under the current rules, the TD must define and mark its direction with a painted line, string, or another marker. When you find an object wrapped in tape or ribbon with nothing on the tee sign explaining it, treat that as an incomplete marking and ask before you throw.

## How to Read the Arrow

Signs with arrows are the most common way a mando is marked, and the arrow points to the side of the object your disc must legally pass. An arrow pointing left on a tree means your throw has to go left of that tree. The word mando itself is short for "mandatory."

Most mandos point left or right. Some point down, and you throw below the object the sign is attached to. A double mando forces you between two objects. Triple mandos, like the archway setup used at the United States Disc Golf Championship, require you to thread through a gate-shaped set of objects. Your event's caddie book can spell this out plainly, as in the example from the UDisc blog: "On hole 14, the first big pine tree is a mandatory object."

You can't assume the direction of the restricted plane anymore. The PDGA states that the direction the restricted space extends from a mandatory object "must be defined and marked by the TD using a painted line, string, or other marker." So if you see tape or ribbon on an object with no clear direction on the tee sign, check the course rules before you throw. I would treat an unmarked mando as unplayable until you find the marker, not as a free pass.

Picture a vertical sheet of paper. Per the PDGA, "Think of the mandatory as a vertical sheet of paper on the 'wrong' side of the mandatory object. If your disc punches a hole in that sheet of paper, you've missed the mandatory." The rule defines this as the restricted plane under 804.01.B. Only part of the disc has to enter it, and where the disc finally lands doesn't matter.

## What Happens If You Miss One

Missing a mando costs you one penalty throw on that hole. The PDGA Official Rules put it plainly in 804.01.C: "If part of a thrown disc clearly enters into a restricted plane, the player receives one penalty throw."

Two details trip people up.

First, one throw can only earn one missed mando penalty. Rule 801.02.I makes it clear that any single "throw or action" can only result in a single penalty, so a shot that clips through two or three mandos still costs one stroke. You then follow the rules for the first mando you missed.

Second, where the disc lands is irrelevant. It can finish in bounds, out of bounds, or on the legal side of the mando object. All that matters is whether part of the thrown disc entered the restricted plane. The PDGA Rules School describes the restricted plane as a vertical sheet of paper on the wrong side of the mando object. Punch a hole in that sheet and you have missed it.

If you realize mid-flight that your shot is heading into the restricted plane, you can abandon the throw and rethrow from the same spot, per 809.01. That option costs one throw, same as the missed mando penalty, but it lets you keep your original lie instead of playing from wherever the bad shot settles.

One last note. You can't reach over the restricted plane and drop your disc on the far side to dodge the mando.

## Drop Zones

A drop zone is a marked area you play from after missing a mando, but only when the tournament director has designated one. If no drop zone exists, the fallback is simple: "If no drop zone has been designated, the lie for the next throw is the previous lie." That comes straight from the mando rules under 804.01.

So you walk back and throw again from where you just threw. Same spot, one penalty stroke added. On a long hole, that stings.

### When OB and Mandos Collide

Say your drive crosses an out-of-bounds line and then clips the restricted plane on the same throw. Which rule wins? The UDisc rules breakdown is blunt about it: "in the battle of OB versus mandos, mandos win." You follow mando rules for your next throw, whether that means the drop zone or the previous lie. Traveling over or through an OB area isn't a violation. Only coming to rest there is.

### One Throw, One Penalty

A single throw can only earn one penalty stroke, per 801.02.I. Miss two mandos with the same throw and you still add just one. You then play from the drop zone or previous lie tied to the first mando you missed.

If the tee sign is unclear about whether a drop zone exists, I would play the previous lie. It's the safer read, and it's the default the rules give you.

## Casual Play vs Tournament

Mandos are course rules, not tournament conditions. If a sign, ribbon, or painted line marks a mandatory object on your local layout, it applies on a quiet Tuesday solo round exactly as it does in a sanctioned event. The sign is part of the hole. I would treat a mando the same way I treat an OB line, which is to say I follow it whether or not anyone is watching.

In tournaments, the caddie book or players' meeting defines each mando. That is where double and triple mandos get spelled out, along with any drop zone.

### When the Marking Is Unclear

Tournament directors set drop zones and mando directions. PDGA Rules School notes that under the current rules the direction the restricted space extends from a mandatory object "must be defined and marked by the TD using a painted line, string, or other marker." It's no longer assumed. So when you find tape or ribbon on a tree with no arrow on the tee sign, check the course rules or ask. If no direction is marked, you have nothing to follow.

My take: casual players should still call their own missed mandos. People often say mandos only matter in leagues and tournaments, and I think that's wrong. You're practicing the hole you'll eventually play for a score. A mando you ignore in practice is one you'll miss when it counts.

## FAQ

### What happens if you miss a mando in disc golf?

According to the PDGA Official Rules of Disc Golf (804.01), a throw has missed the mandatory when part of the disc clearly entered the restricted space. Rule 804.01.C gives one penalty throw for this infraction. If you abandon the throw, you can rethrow from the same spot at the cost of a one-throw penalty (809.01).
