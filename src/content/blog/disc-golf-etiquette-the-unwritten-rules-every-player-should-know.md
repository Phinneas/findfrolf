Disc golf etiquette is the set of behaviors that keep a round fair, safe and quick for everyone on the course, whether you are playing a first casual round or a sanctioned event. The short version: don't throw when it might hurt or distract someone, help find lost discs, keep quiet when others are throwing, and leave the course cleaner than you found it.

You'll run into a few situations often: playing through, lost disc protocol, music on the course, pets, and course maintenance. Here's what the PDGA rules actually say when a courtesy call gets made.

## Playing Through: When to Offer, When to Wait

If your smaller group or solo round catches a bigger, slower group, offer them the play through at the next tee. Keep it simple: "Mind if we play through?" Most groups wave you ahead. The whole exchange takes less time than the hole you would otherwise wait on.

### Tournaments Are Different

Don't ask to play through in a PDGA event. You play in your assigned order, and the PDGA advises players to "not ask to play through in a tournament." If your card is slow, the fix is pace, not passing.

### Never Throw Into the Group Ahead

Firing a drive at a group still putting is the fastest way to start a fight, and it's a rule breach. A player must not "throw if the throw might injure someone or distract another player" (812.A.1). Wait until the hole is clear.

### Stay Behind the Thrower

Walk down the fairway past whoever is throwing and that's a courtesy violation. Rule 812.A.3.e covers "advancing beyond the away player." If you want to scout your line, wait until everyone in the group has thrown.

## Lost Disc Protocol: Finding, Helping, Returning

### Help Look, Even When It's Not Yours

Helping to find a lost disc isn't a favor. It's an expectation, and failing to help is listed among the actions that can draw a courtesy violation under 812.B.1.a. The same rule set says players are expected to watch the other members of the group throw, both to ensure rules compliance and to help find discs (812.B.2). That second part matters more than people think. If you watched the flight, you know roughly where it crossed and where it kicked, which turns a ten-minute search into a two-minute one.

### How Long Is Reasonable

A reasonable amount of time to retrieve a disc is allowed if it doesn't cause an undue delay (802.03.G). That's a retrieval allowance, not an open search window. My read is that if the group has walked the likely line twice and nobody has a bead on it, the disc is gone and the thrower should take the penalty and move on. The common advice to "search until you find it" is wrong in a group setting. You're spending other people's round.

### Lost Versus Out of Bounds

These are different situations, and mixing them up costs strokes. A disc declared out of bounds can't be treated as a lost disc, so the procedures in 805.03 don't apply. If you saw it cross the line, play it as OB. Only when the disc can't be found at all do the lost disc rules come into play.

### When You Find Someone Else's Disc

Check for a name and number. If there is one, send a message and arrange a handoff at the course or a local shop. My pick is to leave it at the course lost and found only if you can't reach the owner after a genuine attempt, and never to quietly pocket a disc you could return. Ghosting a found disc is the fastest way to earn a reputation in a small local scene.

## Music, Pets, and Other Course Companions

Speakers are the running debate on every course. My read of the rules is that volume is yours to choose, but timing isn't. Move or talk during a stranger's backswing and you've earned the same call.

### Smoke, litter, and gear

The rules cover more than throws. The same section says a player must not leave equipment where it may distract other players or interfere with a thrown disc (812.A.4). Bags, stools, and water bottles belong behind the tee line, not beside the flight path.

### Dogs on the course

Pet policy varies by course and park, and I wouldn't assume a course that welcomed dogs last season still does. Leashed and cleaned up after is the baseline. Some courses and many tournaments ban dogs outright, so check the course signage or the event page before you load the car. If dogs are barred and yours is already with you, one of you sits out the round.

When the signs say nothing, ask a local. Regulars know which parks enforce leash rules and which look the other way, and they'll tell you before a ranger does.

## Respect the Course: Maintenance and Leave-No-Trace

Leave the course better than you found it. That starts with carrying out everything you carried in.

### Litter and Graffiti

No littering of any kind. Wrappers, bottles, cigarette butts, all of it goes home with you. Rule 812.A.5 lists littering, cigarette butts included, as a courtesy violation. Graffiti counts too. Writing your name on a basket you aced is vandalism, not a trophy.

### Branches and Obstacles

Don't break off branches or damage plants and equipment to improve your lie. If a small branch blocks your putt, too bad. Play around it.

Here's where players get confused. You can move loose debris small enough to qualify as a casual obstacle under 803.01.B. A branch too big to be loose debris stays put. The PDGA's rules Q&A gives a useful test: a branch large enough to physically prevent a legal stance is too big, because it's not loose debris. If that branch stops you from taking a legal stance behind your marker, you may mark a new lie behind it under 803.02.B.

### Report Damage

If a basket is bent or a teepad is cracked, tell whoever maintains the course. Local clubs and parks departments fix what they know about.

My take: the "too bad" rule bothers people, and it shouldn't. The branch was there before your round. Play the lie you get.

## The Disc Golfer's Code: Play Smart, Respect, Represent

The PDGA's Disc Golfer's Code comes down to three commitments: Play Smart, Respect the Course, and Represent the Sport. They cover the same ground as the courtesy rules, just in plainer language.

At sanctioned events, the standard is formal. Section 3.03 of the Competition Manual requires that "all players must adhere to a professional standard of sporting ethics, courtesy, and integrity while participating in a PDGA-sanctioned event and when commenting to the media." That last clause catches people out. What you post about a cardmate after the round is covered too.

Courtesy violations carry real weight. A player receives a warning for the first violation of any courtesy rule, and each subsequent violation in the same round incurs one penalty throw. Repeated violations may result in disqualification by the Tournament Director. If a call feels wrong, you can briefly state your opinion, but then the rest of the group makes the call.

Casual and league rounds aren't tournaments, and I wouldn't run them like one. If a newer player doesn't know a rule, explain it once and let the round continue. Correct less, demonstrate more. The common advice to "call everything" is wrong for casual play. Nobody learned the rules from being policed on hole three.

Take correction well when it comes your way. You were in their shoes once.

## Pace of Play and Group Responsibilities

Slow play is the most common complaint in league rounds, and most of it comes from not knowing whose turn it's. PDGA rules state that you have 30 seconds once it's your turn. That clock starts when the away player has thrown and you are clear to step up, not when you finish your pre-shot routine.

### Scoring Is a Group Job

Failing to keep score properly is a courtesy violation under 812.B.1.c. After each hole, the player keeping the official scorecard reports their own score first, then asks each other player to report theirs. Announcing your own number before asking keeps the card moving and avoids the awkward pause where four people wait for someone else to speak.

Groups should share scoring duties and carry two independent scoring methods, like the Digital Scorecard plus a paper backup, then compare them before turning anything in. If the two records disagree on a hole, sort it out on the spot while everyone still remembers the throws. Once the card is signed, that window closes.

### Watch the Shots

Under 812.B.2, players are expected to watch the other members of the group throw, both to catch rules issues and to help find discs. Standing behind the tee watching each drive is the easiest habit to build. If a disc kicks into the rough, someone already saw where it crossed.

If a card falls behind and the group ahead waves you through, take it. In a sanctioned event, don't ask.

## FAQ

### What is the 35 rule in disc golf?

There is no 35 rule in the PDGA Official Rules. I checked the rulebook and the number doesn't appear, so treat anyone quoting a "35 rule" with suspicion. The likely mix-up is the 30 second rule, which is real. According to Disc Golf United's etiquette guide, the PDGA rules state that you have 30 seconds once it's your turn. That clock covers your decision and your throw, and it exists to keep the card moving. If someone in your group quotes a 35 rule at you, ask them which section of the rulebook it comes from. Courtesy rules live in section 812, and a made-up number isn't one of them.

### What is the golden rule of disc golf?

Play smart and respect the course. Those components come from the Disc Golfer's Code, which the PDGA Board of Directors devised in 2009, and the PDGA's "Playing Disc Golf By the Code" article frames them as the guidelines that protect the integrity of the game. In practice it means never throwing into a blind area or when players or park patrons are within range, and not taking off down the fairway until everyone has thrown. Off the course, the PDGA says it means sharing disc golf positively with friends, family, and online. If you remember one thing, make it this: the course and the people on it come before your score.

### Found Disc Etiquette?

A found disc isn't yours to keep, and the rules don't require you to hunt down the owner. The courtesy rules put the obligation on the group of the player who lost it. Under 812.B.1.a, a player can be called for a courtesy violation for failing to help find a lost disc, and 812.B.2 expects players to watch the other members of the group throw to ensure rules compliance and to help find discs. So the etiquette runs both ways. If you find a disc with a name and number, a quick text costs you nothing. If it's unmarked and has clearly been sitting for a season, most players I know would call it fair game. My own view is that a text is always the better move.

### How to properly throw a disc golf?

Proper throwing starts with your feet, not your arm. The PDGA's "Six Things to Know Before Your First PDGA Tournament" lays out the stance rules. When you tee off, at least one foot needs to be touching the teepad at the moment you release the disc. No supporting point, whether that's an arm, a leg or your head, can touch anything else. After you tee off, the rule stays the same, except contact with the lie replaces contact with the teepad. To throw from a lie, you take a stance under 802.07, which just means positioning your body to make the throw. Inside the circle, closer than 10 meters, a little under 33 feet, your feet need to stay on or behind the lie the whole time.
