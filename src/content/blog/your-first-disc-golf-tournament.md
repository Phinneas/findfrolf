The first tournament is the scariest round of disc golf you'll ever play — and, once it's over, the one you'll be glad you played. Here's the honest truth from anyone who's been on a card: **tournaments are friendlier than they look.** The guy with the tournament bag and the practiced x-step is usually the nicest person on the course, and the player who helps you find your disc is the one you'll be grouped with next time. This guide walks you through the whole thing so your first event feels like a league night with stakes, not a trial.

## Step 1: Find the right first tournament

Start small. The ideal first event is:

- **A local club event or mini** — $10–20, no membership, no ratings, just a fun competitive round with people you'll see again.
- **A PDGA B-tier or C-tier in a recreational division** — sanctioned, but with divisions for every level, including Novice and Recreational where nobody is bombing 450-foot drives.
- **At a course you already know.** Playing your first tournament on a course you've played ten times removes half the variables.

Where to find them: local disc golf club pages, UDisc event listings, and the course pages of your local courses — [use the finder](/finder) to find the courses near you, then check the events on the ones you like. If you see "Novice" or "Recreational" divisions on the flyer, that's your event.

## Step 2: What happens on the morning of the event

The tournament morning follows a rhythm you'll pick up in five minutes:

1. **Check-in** — arrive 30–45 minutes early, confirm your registration, grab your player's pack (usually a disc or two — that alone can cover the entry fee).
2. **The players meeting** — the tournament director runs through course rules, OB lines, mandos, and any course-specific quirks. This is the only time anyone explains the rules, so listen.
3. **Card assignments** — you'll be grouped with 3–4 other players. You don't choose your card; you get one, and the luck of the draw is part of the fun.
4. **Tee off** — cards typically start in waves from hole 1 or on a shotgun start (every card starts on a different hole).

Your card will be a mix of experience levels, and the experienced players will almost always offer to keep score and explain the local rules. Let them. That's the culture.

## Step 3: The format — it's simpler than it sounds

**You're playing stroke play: count every throw, lowest total wins.** Pars barely matter — what matters is your total throws across the round.

**Everyone keeps someone else's score.** It's called "marking" — you write the scores for the player on your card, and someone writes yours. It's the honor system made visible, and it's why the game works without referees. At the end of the round, your card confirms each other's totals and signs.

**Rules you'll actually use:** the furthest-out throws first, OB and mandos (marked with signs or flags — the players meeting covers them), and you may not move or break vegetation to improve your lie. For everything else, [the rules are simpler than you think](/blog/disc-golf-rules-explained) — and asking your card is always allowed.

## Step 4: What to bring

- **Water** — more than you think, especially on open courses.
- **Snacks** — a tournament round is 3–5 hours; bonk is real.
- **A mini marker** — a small disc-shaped marker to mark your lie (some players mark with anything flat).
- **A towel** — for dew, rain, or just wiping your hands.
- **A spare of your favorite disc** — the one you'd hate to lose is the one you'll lose.
- **Cash or a card** — entry fee day-of if you haven't pre-paid, plus merch. Tournament shirts are the real trophy.
- **An extra pair of socks** — undramatic and invaluable.

## Step 5: Divisions — where you belong

PDGA events run divisions from Novice (the entry level) up through Open (the pros). **If there's a Novice or Recreational division, play it.** Nobody there has anything to prove, the scoring is forgiving, and you'll actually have fun — which is the point. Move up when the local players start asking you to.

The cardinal rule: don't sandbag (play below your level to win) and don't jump up too far (play above your level to prove something). The community notices both — and neither is worth it.

## Step 6: The honest expectations

You will probably shoot **worse than your casual rounds**. Nerves add strokes, tournament pins are placed in harder spots, and the pace is different. That's normal — it happens to everyone, every time, at every level. The players who look calm on the tee are just better at hiding it.

What actually happens at your first tournament:

- You'll learn more about your own game in four hours than in a month of solo rounds.
- You'll meet the people who run the local scene — and they'll remember your name.
- You'll probably finish somewhere in the middle or the bottom, and you won't care, because you'll already be planning the next one.

## The post-round ritual

Stick around after your round. There's usually food, the awards ceremony (where every division winner gets called up — including last place in Novice, sometimes with a cheer), and the general debrief where everyone compares rounds. **This is where you actually join the community** — the disc golf in your city runs through these people, and they love new faces.

When you're ready, find your local courses with the [disc golf course finder](/finder), check out what's happening near you, and sign up for something small. The first tournament is the scariest part of the sport — and it's also the best part.
