No, disc golf is not expensive. It is one of the cheapest sports you can take up. Most courses charge nothing, and one disc in the $10 to $20 range is enough to play your first round.

So is disc golf expensive? Only if you want it to be. The costs climb when you buy a lot of discs, join leagues or enter tournaments. This guide covers what you pay for courses, starter gear and competitive play, and how to keep the total low.

## Course fees: most courses are free

The biggest cost in ball golf is the green fee. Disc golf mostly skips it. UDisc's 2026 Disc Golf Growth Report found that 89% of disc golf courses are free to play. The same report counts 11,165 courses in the US. That works out to roughly nine of every ten courses with no fee at all.

Most free courses sit in city and county parks. The parks department or a local club installs the baskets. Then anyone can walk on and play. There is no tee time and no clubhouse. Nobody checks a ticket.

### Pay-to-play courses

The other courses charge a fee, and these tend to be private. You see them on farms, at resorts, at ski hills in summer and on shared ball golf properties. Disc Golf United puts most pay-to-play fees anywhere from a few dollars up to $10 to $15 a round. Courses that share land with a ball golf course can cost close to a ball golf round, and some charge extra for a cart.

A fee is not a bad sign. Pay-to-play courses often have better upkeep, cleaner fairways and real tee pads. I think a good private course is worth the fee once you know you like the game. For a first round, start free. You can find one near you with our guide to [finding free public disc golf courses](/blog/free-disc-golf-how-to-find-public-courses/), or browse [the best free disc golf courses in America](/blog/best-free-disc-golf-courses/).

### Check before you drive

A few public parks charge a parking or entry fee even when the course itself is free. State parks are the usual case. Check the park's page before you go so the fee is not a surprise at the gate.

## Starter gear: one disc is enough

You do not need a set to start. One disc will carry you through a full round. UDisc estimates the cost of a first disc at about $15. Disc Golf United puts a single new disc at $10 to $20, and a few discs at around $30 to $50 total.

### Which disc to buy first

If you buy one disc, make it a midrange or a putter. Both fly straight and slow. They forgive a bad release. Drivers look like the obvious pick because they promise distance. But a fast driver needs arm speed most new players do not have yet. Thrown slowly, it fades hard left (for a right-handed backhand) and lands short.

This is the advice I disagree with most. New players hear "get a driver for distance" all the time. I would skip the driver at first. A midrange thrown well goes farther than a driver thrown badly. Our guide to [what discs you need for disc golf](/blog/disc-golf-discs-for-beginners/) breaks down the three disc types and which to buy in what order.

### Starter sets

Brands sell three-disc starter sets with a driver, a midrange and a putter. Disc Golf United lists Innova's DX starter set at under $30. That is cheaper than buying the three discs one at a time. The catch is the plastic. Starter sets use base plastic, which scuffs and changes flight faster than premium plastic. For a beginner that does not matter much.

### What you can skip

You can skip almost everything else at the start.

- **A bag.** Three discs fit in your hands or a backpack.
- **Special shoes.** Sneakers with grip work fine on most courses.
- **A practice basket.** Most parks let you putt on the course baskets.
- **Rangefinders, carts and grip aids.** These are for regular players.

If you do want a bag, small ones that hold about 4 to 8 discs usually cost $10 to $30. Medium bags for 8 to 14 discs run about $30 to $50, according to Disc Golf Fanatic.

## What costs more as you get into it

Disc golf stays cheap if you keep it casual. It gets more expensive as you play more often and start competing. Here is where the money goes.

### More discs and better plastic

Most regular players carry far more than three discs. They add drivers for different shots and keep backups. Premium plastic costs more than base plastic and lasts longer. Lost discs are a real cost too, since water and thick rough claim discs on many courses.

### Bigger bags

A bag that holds 15 or more discs usually costs $50 to $250, Disc Golf Fanatic reports. Custom and high-end bags can go well past that. A big bag is a choice, not a need. Plenty of strong players carry a small bag on purpose.

### Leagues

Weekly leagues are the cheapest way to play competitive rounds. Disc Golf United puts club and league rounds at $5 to $25 each. Part of the fee usually goes into a payout pool or an ace pot. Leagues also help you improve faster because you play with better players every week.

### Tournaments

Sanctioned tournaments cost more. Disc Golf United says smaller PDGA events start around $40, and the biggest pro events reach several hundred dollars. Amateur entry fees come back to players as prizes and player packs. PDGA rules require amateur payouts of at least 85% of net entry fees at C-tier events. That share rises to 100% at B-tier and 110% at A-tier. So part of what you pay often comes back as discs and gear. If you're thinking about your first event, start with our guide to [your first disc golf tournament](/blog/your-first-disc-golf-tournament/).

### PDGA membership

You do not need a PDGA membership to play in most local events. Non-members pay a small extra fee at C-tier events, according to the PDGA's events guide. Membership is an annual fee that waives that charge and gives you an official rating. Check the PDGA site for current rates. It only makes sense once you play tournaments regularly.

## Ways to keep disc golf cheap

Most of the cost in disc golf is optional. These habits keep it low.

### Buy used discs

Many disc shops sell used discs. Disc Golf United puts the average used putter at $5 to $10. A used disc flies close to new. Online marketplaces and local disc golf groups also sell used discs.

### Borrow before you buy

Ask a friend who plays to lend you discs for a round. Some public libraries now lend disc golf kits for free, UDisc notes. Check your local library before you buy anything.

### Play free courses first

Stick to free courses until you know you like the game. With about nine in ten courses free, most players can do this for years. Start with courses built for new players. Our list of [the best beginner-friendly disc golf courses in the US](/blog/best-beginner-friendly-disc-golf-courses/) is a good place to look.

### Write your name on your discs

Put your name and phone number on the back of every disc. Lost discs come back far more often when someone can reach you. It is the cheapest way to stop buying replacements.

### Add discs slowly

Buy one disc at a time and only when you know what shot you are missing. Most beginners buy too many discs too early. A small set you know well beats a big bag you do not.

## FAQ

### Is it free to play disc golf?

Usually, yes. UDisc found that 89% of disc golf courses are free to play. Most free courses are in public parks. The rest charge a fee, often from a few dollars up to $10 to $15 a round.

### Is disc golf a cheap hobby?

Yes. It is one of the cheapest sports to start. One disc at $10 to $20 and a free course is all you need. Costs only climb if you buy many discs, join leagues or enter tournaments.

### Is disc golf expensive to play?

Not at the casual level. A round on a free course costs nothing after your first disc. Competitive play costs more. League rounds run about $5 to $25, and tournaments start around $40.

### Is disc golf beginner friendly?

Yes. You can play with one disc, there is no tee time, and most courses are free. Courses with short holes and open fairways are the easiest place to start. A midrange or putter is the most forgiving first disc.

### How many discs do you need to start?

One. A midrange or a putter is enough for a full round. Many players add a driver and a putter after a few rounds. A three-disc starter set covers all three types for under $30.
